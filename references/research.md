# Research procedure

## Frame the decision

1. State the question, intended audience, and decision the answer must support.
2. Separate required facts from interpretations, recommendations, and optional context.
3. Define the relevant population, geography, period, units, and comparison basis.
4. Resolve only ambiguities that could change the result; otherwise state a reasonable assumption.
5. Select a stopping condition: enough evidence for the requested decision, with material uncertainty exposed.

## Select evidence

- Use available native search, retrieval, and connected-source capabilities.
- Apply the relevant installed research or domain skill for its specific workflow.
- Prefer original documents, official records, direct measurements, and primary research.
- Use secondary sources to locate originals or explain context; check consequential claims against primary evidence.
- Match the evidence to the claim: a product announcement does not establish independent effectiveness.
- Use current sources for unstable facts, recommendations, policies, prices, and high-stakes guidance.
- Check both publication date and the date of the underlying event or observation.
- Distinguish the latest available report from a report describing the present period.
- Do not interpret an inaccessible page, empty search, or missing record as evidence of absence.

## Retrieve deliberately

1. Start with the most discriminating terms, identifiers, and likely primary sources.
2. Batch independent searches; keep follow-up retrieval dependent on what the first results establish.
3. Open the relevant source section before relying on a summary or search snippet.
4. Record the source title or identifier, version or date, locator, and retrieval time when freshness matters.
5. Capture the passage, table, or calculation needed to support each material claim.
6. Expand the search only to resolve a concrete evidence gap, contradiction, or coverage limitation.

## Evaluate and reconcile

- Track material claims as supported, qualified, contradicted, or unresolved.
- Assess source proximity, methods, sample limits, incentives, and applicability to the question.
- Compare conflicting claims using their definitions, time periods, denominators, and methods first.
- Prefer stronger applicable evidence; do not vote across sources or count duplicated reporting as corroboration.
- Preserve substantive disagreement when the available evidence cannot resolve it.
- Label inference explicitly and show which established facts support it.
- Treat an unsupported claim as unresolved; do not silently upgrade plausible wording into fact.
- Recalculate important derived quantities from cited inputs and keep assumptions visible.

## Synthesize for use

1. Lead with the answer or decision implication at the confidence the evidence permits.
2. Explain the few findings that materially change the decision.
3. Separate observed facts, inference, and proposed action in the wording.
4. State important uncertainty alongside the affected claim, with its practical consequence.
5. Offer a next check only when its result could change the recommendation.
6. Use a comparison table or chart only when it makes the evidence easier to assess.

## Attribute and finish

- Cite the source actually examined, close to the supported claim, using the environment's citation format.
- Make each citation support the whole nearby claim; split claims that require different evidence.
- Respect quote and source word limits; paraphrase accurately without reproducing excessive source content.
- Preserve source qualifications, scope, and relevant negative findings when paraphrasing.
- Check that every consequential factual claim has adequate support or an explicit limitation.
- Stop when the decision is adequately supported; do not add searches merely to increase source count.
- Before handoff or compaction, record the question, source versions, supported findings, unresolved conflicts, and remaining completion criteria.
